amux vs herdr: Which Should Run Your Coding Agents?

herdr and amux both keep coding agents running after you walk away and let you answer them from your phone. Compared on what each one is, where agents run, coordinating work, phone access, license and price. Updated November 2026.

Short answer: use herdr if you want a free, Apache-2.0 workspace for your agents' terminals on macOS, Linux or Windows, with every SSH machine in one sidebar, a phone app, and an app you reshape by asking your agent. Use amux if you want the layer above the terminals: a shared task board agents claim work from, a scheduler, recovery from usage limits, email and browser automation, and an always-on server you can hand to a team. The two also work together: amux can run its workers inside herdr instead of tmux.

What are amux and herdr?

amux is a self-hosted control plane for running a team of AI coding agents from one dashboard or your phone. It launches and monitors parallel Claude Code, Codex, Gemini CLI, OpenCode and Ollama sessions, with a shared kanban board agents claim tasks from, agent-to-agent messages, shared memory, a scheduler, email and browser automation. It is one Rust binary, runs on any Mac or Linux machine, and is source-available under MIT + Commons Clause.

herdr calls itself "the runtime your coding agents live on". Your agents run in real terminals inside herdr-core, a small background server, so they keep going when you close the window or lose your SSH connection. Every pane is marked working, blocked or idle, every machine you can reach over SSH joins one sidebar, and agents can start, prompt and wait on each other through its CLI. There are two clients: the original Herdr TUI and a newer native app for macOS, Linux and Windows whose layout, keys and features you change by asking your agent. It is free and open source under Apache 2.0, with about 51,500 GitHub stars. Ovis, a separate MIT project, shows herdr's agents as chats in a browser and on iPhone and Android.

Are they the same kind of tool?

Not quite, and the difference decides most choices. herdr owns your agents' terminals: where they run, how they survive a closed window, how you see which one is stuck, and how one agent hands a task to another. It stays close to the terminal and leaves the work itself to you and your agents.

amux sits a layer above. Work lives on a board, agents claim cards and message each other, a scheduler starts recurring jobs, and usage limits pause delivery until the provider resets instead of failing the task. The same server also sends email through the Gmail API, drives a browser with your saved sign-ins and keeps a record of what each worker did. If what you want is a better place for your terminals, herdr is the lighter tool. If you want agents to work through a queue while you are away, that is what amux is for.

Can I use amux with herdr?

Yes. amux runs each worker in tmux by default, and can run a worker inside herdr instead: set CC_BACKEND=herdr on that worker, or AMUX_BACKEND=herdr for the whole server. amux then keeps the board, scheduler and dashboard, and herdr holds the terminals.

Which is better from your phone?

Both have real phone apps. herdr's apps for iPhone, iPad and Android show who needs you and open the real terminal; they connect straight to your machines over SSH, with no account. Ovis shows each herdr agent's transcript as a chat, turns every question into an approval card, and reaches your computer through an end-to-end encrypted relay or your own network such as Tailscale.

amux has a native iPhone and iPad app and a web app you can install on Android or any browser. The phone shows every agent at once with its live terminal, the task board, and controls to answer, approve, restart or reassign.

How much do they cost?

herdr and Ovis are free. amux is free to self-host and uses the subscriptions or API keys you already have. amux Cloud, if you would rather not keep a machine on, costs $39/mo for Solo (4 vCPU, 8 GB), $69/mo for Team (8 vCPU, 16 GB) and $129/mo for Max, for a dedicated server at yourname.amux.io; model usage stays on your own accounts.

When should you choose amux or herdr?

Choose herdr if

  • You want a free, Apache-2.0 home for your agents' terminals, on macOS, Linux or Windows.
  • Your agents run on several machines and you want them in one sidebar over SSH.
  • You coordinate the work yourself, and want agents to hand tasks to each other from the terminal.
  • You want to reshape the app itself by asking your agent.

Choose amux if

  • You want agents working through a shared task board, claiming cards and resuming after usage limits.
  • You want a scheduler, email and browser automation next to your agents, to run business tasks as well as code.
  • You want teammates on the same server and board.
  • You want agents working with your laptop closed on a server someone else keeps up: amux Cloud gives you a dedicated always-on server from $39/mo.

FAQ

What is herdr?

herdr is a free, Apache-2.0 workspace for coding agents. Agents run in real terminals inside herdr-core, a background server, so they keep going when you close the window. Every pane shows whether its agent is working, blocked or idle, machines you reach over SSH join one sidebar, and there are a TUI, a native app for macOS, Linux and Windows, and phone apps.

Is amux an alternative to herdr?

For some jobs. herdr is the better fit if you want a light, free home for your agents' terminals across your machines. amux is for running a team of agents on shared work: a task board, a scheduler, recovery from usage limits, email and browser automation, and a server you can share with teammates.

Can amux run on top of herdr?

Yes. amux uses tmux by default and can run a worker inside herdr instead, with CC_BACKEND=herdr on the worker or AMUX_BACKEND=herdr for the whole server.

What is Ovis?

Ovis is a separate, free project that shows herdr's agents as chats on the desktop, in any browser and on iPhone and Android, with approval cards and the live terminal a tap away. Its server is open source under MIT.

Is amux open source?

amux is source-available under MIT + Commons Clause: the code is on GitHub and free to self-host, including for commercial work you build with it; the Commons Clause means you cannot sell amux itself. herdr is open source under Apache 2.0.
